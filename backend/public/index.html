LIMITS

What it won't do.

An honest list, because you're handing this to a child.

It can be wrong

It works from a language model, so it will sometimes be wrong or out of date. An answer is the start of a conversation with you, not a source of record.

It doesn't chat back

It answers and stops. No follow-up question, no prompt to keep going, no greeting when your child picks it up. It builds no picture of them over time.

It can't help in an emergency

It is not a phone, an alarm or a tracker. It cannot call anyone, it does not know where your child is, and it only works on your home Wi-Fi.

It won't answer above your child's age

Age grounding

You set your child's age, and every answer is written for it — the words, the examples, and how much it assumes they already know. A five-year-old gets a sentence about why the sky is blue; an eleven-year-old gets the actual reason. The same question, answered differently, because they are different children.

Review after the answer

A second pass reads each answer once it has already been spoken and flags what shouldn't have been said — content above the age on the device, anything touching self-harm, and similar. When it flags something you get a notification, with the question that caused it, and the device can stop answering until you say otherwise. Be clear about what that is and isn't: a safety net that catches things after the fact, not a filter that stops a child hearing them.

MY PROMISE

Three things I've decided, and why.

Every one of them costs me something. They're here because the reasoning is the part you can actually check — and a promise you can read is worth more than one you have to take on trust.

Pay per curiosity, not per month

I could charge $9.99 every month and cap the device at so many questions a day. Most of this category does. But children aren't monthly. A child hits a dinosaur week and asks forty questions a day, then barely touches it until something at school sets them off again. A monthly plan charges the quiet weeks to pay for the loud ones, and then has to cap the loud ones anyway.

So I meter it instead. You buy tokens, your child spends them at whatever pace their curiosity runs, and a month where nothing catches their interest costs you nothing. No cap on the good days. If they want to spend a Saturday on volcanoes, that's the whole point of the thing.

And when the tokens run out: the device says so, out loud, and stops answering until you top it up. It doesn't brick, it doesn't lose the history, and there is nothing to cancel because there was never anything recurring. I'd rather say it here than bury it in terms you find out about later.

Why I don't allow BYOK (Bring Your Own Key)

Devices like this sometimes ask you to open an AI account and paste in your own key. It looks cheaper, and it looks like it keeps me out of your family's data. It would work — the technical part is straightforward. The case against it is legal and commercial, not technical, and it comes down to two things.

  • Personal AI keys aren't licensed for this. Google's terms for individual developer keys bar using them in any service aimed at children. That restriction is about what the service is, not how old the account holder is — so a parent's own key doesn't get around it. Answering questions for children needs a commercial agreement written for that purpose, which is what I'm putting in place, and it isn't something a personal key can offer. It also wouldn't move anything onto you: I'd still be the one collecting and transcribing your child's speech, so the responsibility for it doesn't travel with the credit card.
  • The free option trains on what your child says. Without a card attached, Google's terms let them use what's submitted to improve their products, and say human reviewers may read it. Given the choice, most people would pick free — and a child's questions would end up in a training set, where no delete button of mine could ever reach them.

So I pay for the answering on a commercial account, on terms that keep your child's questions out of anyone's training data, and pass the cost through as tokens.

If you stop paying, you still own a computer

Inside is a stock ESP32-S3 — a Waveshare ESP32-S3-Touch-AMOLED-1.43C, the same module anyone can buy. Not a locked board, not a custom chip that only talks to my servers. It has 8 MB of flash, 8 MB of PSRAM, two microphones, a speaker, a round AMOLED touch screen and a USB-C port.

Which means the failure mode of this product is not a brick. If you run out of tokens and never buy more, you are holding a fully functional, reprogrammable computer — one that plugs into USB-C and takes new firmware from the standard Espressif toolchain like any other dev board. Turn it into a clock, a step counter, a music player, a weather display, or a school project. That is not a loophole I forgot to close; it is a stock part I chose deliberately.

And I'll help you do it. Tell me you want to reset yours and I'll send you the whole route: how to put the board into download mode, how to erase it, and how to get the first project running on it. Not a link to a datasheet — actual step-by-step instructions written for someone who has never flashed a microcontroller before.

With it come real, working STEM projects you can build on this exact hardware with your child — code that compiles and runs, not screenshots of somebody else's. A talking clock. A colour-changing night light. A "how loud is this room?" meter using the two microphones. A step counter. Every one of them is a genuine first electronics project, and the parts are already in your hand.

WHY THIS EXISTS

I built this for my son.

I'm Nishant Vyas. I have a ten-year-old, and he is relentlessly curious — mostly about cars, engines and Lego. Why one engine sounds different from another. What actually makes a car fast. Whether the thing he just built would hold together if it were real. One of our favourite things to do in the car is put Gemini on over Bluetooth and let him ask whatever he's wondering about. We do the same at home with our Google Home.

Those moments are the best part of my week. But I could never hand either of them to him and walk away, for three reasons.

They're always listening. A smart speaker sits there with its microphone open, waiting for a wake word. The data goes somewhere I don't control. I can't tell you what's kept, for how long, or make it go away. And they don't move — a Google Home lives on a kitchen counter, so the question has to wait until he's standing near it.

The obvious answer is to give him a phone. I don't want to. The screen is the whole problem: he'd ask one question and still be there forty minutes later.

So I built the thing I actually wanted. It only listens while a button is held — no wake word, no open microphone. It's small enough to carry into another room. And I decide what's kept and for how long, because I'm the one holding that switch, not a company.

To be straight with you: Watima still sends the question to Google's models to get an answer — that's what makes a good answer possible. The difference is that it only sends when the button is pressed, I pay for the commercial terms that keep your child's words out of anyone's training data, and the history belongs to you to read or erase. That's the part I couldn't get any other way, so I built it.
